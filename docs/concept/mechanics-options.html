tcg-card-sorter · step 2 + 3 options

How the sorter could move cards

Every drawing is a side or top view of the same small machine: about 50 unsleeved cards (63 × 88 mm, ~0.3 mm thick), 5 V over USB-C, backpack size. The card being moved is always drawn in colour.

Part 1

Feeder: get exactly one card off the stack

This is the part other builders struggle with most. The real question is how the machine stops the second card from coming along.

A · Bottom roller + gate

first choice
Good
  • One motor (stepper)
  • Gentle on cards
  • Card shufflers and dispensers work this way
Bad
  • The gap must be set finely, so it needs an adjusting screw
  • New, slippery cards can come along in pairs

B · Top roller + spring

printer style
Good
  • Well known from paper trays
  • Pressure stays even as the stack shrinks
Bad
  • Spring + moving floor = more parts
  • The machine gets taller
  • The spring must be very soft for 50 light cards

C · Push blade

backup choice
Good
  • Best at stopping double feeds
  • One servo, no rubber that wears out
Bad
  • The blade must be metal (a feeler gauge or steel strip). Printed plastic can't be that thin
  • Bent cards slip over the blade
  • Can scuff the card edge

D · Suction cup

skip for v1
Good
  • Very good at separating sticky new cards
Bad
  • Pump + valve + lift arm = three extra parts
  • Loud, and pumps draw a lot of power for 5 V
  • Too big for a backpack
Recommendation for the feeder

Print A as a quick test rig first. If it lets cards through in pairs, add a small rubber pad above the gap, which is how paper printers stop double feeds. Only switch to C if that still fails. The check stays the same: 200 cards, no doubles, no jams.

Part 2

Router: send the card to the right bin

Every option needs one thing first: the card has to lie still under the phone for the photo. A small stop gate (one servo) holds it there and then lets it go.

1 · Swinging chute

first choice
Good
  • Simplest to print and fix
  • One servo for 3–5 bins
  • Easy to see what's going wrong
Bad
  • Bins sit in a fan, so it's wide
  • Cards must slide well, so the chute needs to be steep and smooth (PTFE tape)

2 · Lift with drawers

if 1 is too wide
Good
  • Smallest footprint, so it fits a backpack best
  • Drawers are easy to empty
Bad
  • A lead screw, a guide and the stack weight make it harder to build
  • Taller machine

3 · Turning ring of bins

bulky
Good
  • One stepper, no sliding of the card
  • Adding bins is easy
Bad
  • Round, so the card-sized bins waste a lot of space
  • Bulky in a backpack

4 · Flaps along a ramp

long
Good
  • Very easy to understand and debug
  • Fast
Bad
  • One servo per bin
  • The ramp makes it long: 4 bins ≈ 40 cm
  • Wrong shape for a backpack
Recommendation for the router

Start with 1 · Swinging chute. It's the simplest to get working. Build the cardboard mock-up in step 3 with 4 bins in a fan. If that's too wide for your backpack, switch to 2 · Lift with drawers, which is smaller but harder to build.

Part 3

Swinging chute vs. turning ring, in detail

Both use one motor and 4 bins (3 + reject). The real differences are how the card travels, how big the machine gets, and whether the motor knows where it is.

1 · Swinging chute3 · Turning ring
How the card travelsIt slides down a chute at 35–45°, then drops into the bin.It drops 2–4 cm straight down into the bin under it. No sliding.
Motor1 servo (MG90S)1 stepper (28BYJ-48) + a bearing for the turntable
Does it know where it is?Yes. A servo goes to an angle you give it.No. At start it must find "home", so it needs a second sensor (hall sensor + magnet, ~€1).
Footprint≈ 30 × 24 cm≈ 27 cm round (scan sits inside it)
HeightTaller: the chute needs ~8 cm of drop to reach the outer bins at a steep angle.Lower: only a short drop.
More bins laterHard. The servo turns only ~180°, so the fan gets wider and wider.Easy. Same motor, slightly bigger ring, 6 sectors.
Build effortLower: chute, servo mount, a row of bins.A bit higher: turntable, bearing, centre axle, home sensor.
Main riskThe card stops halfway in the chute (friction, static).The card lands crooked or flips while it falls.
EmptyingPull out a bin from the row.Lift out a bin from the ring.
How to decide: one cardboard test per risk, about 15 minutes

Chute test: fold a cardboard chute, tape PTFE or baking paper on it, set it to 40°, and slide 20 cards down into a box. Do any stop halfway?

Ring test: hold a card flat 3 cm above a 95 × 95 mm box and let it go, 20 times. Do any land crooked or bounce out?

Keep the one that passes. If both pass, the ring wins on size and on adding bins later, and the chute wins on being simpler to build.

Part 4 · your idea

Cross chute: four fixed directions

The bins stand still in a cross around the scan spot. A short chute under the scan spot turns to one of four directions, 90° apart. It keeps what is good about both: the bins don't move, as with the chute, and they sit close, as with the ring.

1+3 · Cross chute

fallback
Good
  • Bins stand still: easy to empty, and the motor never carries cards
  • Short slide (~6 cm), so it's lower than the long chute
  • The empty corners hold the ESP32 and the USB-C board
Bad
  • Four directions need 270° of turning, and a normal servo only does 180°
  • The reject bin under the feeder can only be pulled out like a drawer

The 270° question

pick one
a · 270° servo

These exist but are rarer in the small size. Same code as a normal servo, and it still knows its position. Check before buying.

b · stepper + home sensor

A 28BYJ-48 turns any amount, plus a hall sensor and magnet (~€1) to find the start. Very cheap, and it can go to 6 directions later.

c · 180° servo, 3 bins only

Left, front and right. The reject bin becomes "the card stays on the scan spot and the machine stops". Simplest, but it pauses on every unreadable card.

Lean: b. It's the cheapest, and it also leaves room for more bins later.

The test changes a little

Do the chute test with a short cardboard chute: about 6 cm long at 40°, 20 cards into a box. The ring's drop test is no longer needed for this layout.

Part 5 · your idea

Spit-out: nothing turns, wheels throw the card to a side

The card lands on a small platform over two crossed wheels. One wheel can push it left or right, the other front or back. The platform is smaller than the card, so the card tips off the edge and slides down a short slope into the bin. Concept sketch: sizes are rough.

What gets better

  • No turning chute, no start-position sensor
  • No stop gate: the wheels hold the card still for the photo
  • Same motor count as before (feeder + 2 wheels = 3)
  • Smaller: about 23 × 30 cm

What is new and unproven

  • The wheels must be omni wheels: they drive in one direction and roll freely sideways. Normal rubber wheels would block each other.
  • Grip: a card weighs under 2 g, so a ball has to press it down. The ball hides a small spot of the card from the camera.
  • Twist: the wheels are not exactly under the middle, so the card may turn a little while it leaves.
Test before choosing

Build only the platform: one small gear motor, one omni wheel, one ball on top. Throw 20 cards off it. If every card leaves cleanly and lands in a box, this design beats the cross chute. If cards slip or twist, fall back to the cross chute from Part 4.
